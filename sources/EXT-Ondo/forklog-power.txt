Power Struggle Emerges at Ondo Finance Following Founder’s Death
Ondo Finance faces a power struggle after founder's death.
The mother of the late Ondo Finance founder Nathan Allman has filed a lawsuit in Delaware court seeking control over the RWA project, according to The Block.
Allman passed away in late May at the age of 32. At that time, Ondo’s management announced that Ian De Bode, then the firm’s president, would assume the role of CEO. The cause of death was not disclosed by the project team, nor is it mentioned in court documents, which also lack details on the size of the voting shares owned by the founder.
It is with profound sadness that we announce the unexpected passing of Nathan Allman, Ondo’s founder. Our hearts are with his family and loved ones.
Nate’s brilliance, humility, and drive shaped every part of what Ondo is today. His belief in the power of technology to create a…
According to Caitlin Allman, at the time of his death, her son held the roles of CEO, sole director, and majority shareholder. The company’s charter allowed for two board seats, but one remained vacant.
“When Nate died, there were no directors left,” the statement reads.
The founder’s death paralyzed both management mechanisms. No one could appoint a new CEO or hold a meeting to form a new board. The voting shares were part of the estate, leaving no one with the authority to manage them.
The impasse lasted a month until June 26, when a Hawaii court appointed Caitlin Allman as the administrator of her son’s estate, granting her voting rights.
According to the plaintiff, De Bode took advantage of this interim period. He first cited the charter to declare his automatic transition to CEO. He then used a shareholder agreement to appoint himself as the sole director and began acting independently on behalf of Ondo: hiring consultants, approving bonus payments, and adding an additional board member.
The lawsuit challenges the first link in this chain. Caitlin Allman insists that the charter allowed for the CEO vacancy to be filled only by a board decision, which no longer existed. De Bode’s appointment is invalid, and consequently, all subsequent decisions are void, according to court documents.
Among the specific allegations is an accusation of coercion. The plaintiff claims that De Bode used corporate resources to pressure her into signing documents that would cement his control over the company.
Another point in the lawsuit involves the refusal to provide a list of shareholders with contact information.
After gaining voting rights over the estate’s shares, Caitlin Allman did not immediately remove De Bode, according to documents. Initially, she proposed a peaceful resolution: she joined the board of directors, approved a temporary operational procedure to keep the business running, and confirmed De Bode’s status as president, but not CEO.
De Bode and Ondo’s external lawyers did not accept these decisions. After their refusal, the plaintiff expanded the board from two to four seats, adding Gordon Liao and Tania Towill. Liao declined the appointment for reasons unrelated to the dispute. Towill, Nathan Allman’s sister and a recruiter from Hawaii, agreed, giving Allman a decisive second vote.
On July 24, the two voted to remove De Bode from all positions, including president. On the same day, Allman assumed the roles of board chair, CEO, secretary, and treasurer.
The core of the dispute boils down to a power struggle: Ondo Finance effectively has two centers of control, each claiming legitimacy. In three filed documents, the plaintiff asks the court to determine the lawful owner of control over the company and to prohibit any extraordinary corporate actions until a verdict is reached.
The plaintiff insists on an expedited trial. As long as the question of control remains unresolved, the legal validity of any company decisions—from contracts and expense orders to the issuance of new shares—is at risk.
De Bode rejected the allegations in a conversation with journalists. He stated that the plaintiff’s claims are unfounded and that key stakeholders, including leading investors and the Ondo Foundation, support the company.
“Caitlin Allman’s decision to go to court is regrettable. This path clearly contradicts the interests of the company, its shareholders, the team, and the entire Ondo community,” he said.
The court has yet to make a decision, and all filed documents reflect only the heirs’ position.
